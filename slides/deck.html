Welcome. This workshop is about one habit: never trust a fluent answer you have not checked. Everything shown today is in the public repository, including the notebook, which runs in Google Colab without installation.

The one idea

The LLM proposes.
A curated database decides.

Every demo today turns an LLM answer into checkable claims and checks them against Crossref, PubMed, NCBI Gene, UniProt, PubChem, Wikidata or openFDA.

This is the sentence to remember. Language models are excellent at proposing: drafting, summarising, suggesting. Curated, versioned databases are where facts are decided. The workshop is about wiring those two together.

Part 1 · Why plausible ≠ correct

What a language model does

1

It continues text with likely next tokens, learned from its training data.

2

By default it does not look anything up.

3

Right and wrong answers come out equally fluent. Fluency is not evidence.

No computer science needed. A model that has read millions of methods sections can write a convincing one for an experiment that never happened. Fluency and correctness are produced by the same machinery, so fluency cannot tell you whether an answer is correct.

Two kinds of error

Factuality

The output conflicts with world knowledge.

“BRCA1 is on chromosome 13.” It is on 17; BRCA2 is on 13.

Faithfulness

The output does not follow the given source or instruction.

A summary that adds a number the abstract does not contain.

Huang et al. 2025, ACM TOIS · Ji et al. 2023, ACM Computing Surveys

Both matter in research. Faithfulness errors are especially dangerous in literature work and data extraction, because the source is right there and we assume the model used it.

Why it happens

Long-tail knowledge

Accuracy tracks how often related documents appeared in pre-training. Rare genes and compounds suffer most.

Kandpal et al. 2023, ICML

Guessing is rewarded

Training and benchmark scoring reward a guess over “I don’t know”.

Kalai et al. 2025

Sycophancy

Assistants tend to agree with the view stated in the prompt. Keep your hypothesis out of the question.

Sharma et al. 2024, ICLR

Snowballing

Models defend early errors, yet ChatGPT and GPT-4 recognised 67% and 87% of their own mistakes when asked separately.

Zhang et al. 2024, ICML

Four well-supported causes. The long tail matters most for us: research questions are about rare entities by definition. Sycophancy is the practical one to remember: if you write "explain why X causes Y", the model will usually explain why, whether or not it is true.

The trend is improving but it is not zero, and you cannot tell which 7 percent are wrong without checking. The package result matters for bioinformaticians: installing an LLM-suggested package name is a software supply-chain risk.

Working principle

Use LLMs where errors are cheap to detect, and design your workflow so that errors become cheap to detect.

This is the design goal for every workflow in the rest of the workshop. Identifiers, structured output, quotes and database checks are all ways of making errors cheap to detect.

Part 2 · Mitigation

Five levers, cheapest first

LeverWhat it meansExample
Ask betteridentifiers, JSON, “null if unsure”, neutral wordingDemo 2 prompt

Use toolslet the model query databasesGeneGPT; ChemCrow

Verifycheck every claim against a curated sourcethis notebook

Lewis 2020 · Jin 2024 · M. Bran 2024 · Manakul 2023 · Farquhar 2024 · full list in references.md

The levers stack. Asking better costs nothing. Grounding and tools change where facts come from. Uncertainty measures tell you where to look. Verification is the final gate and is never optional for facts that go into a paper.

Lookup beats recall

0.12

ChatGPT, answering from memory

0.83

GeneGPT, calling NCBI Web APIs

Average score on the GeneTuring genomics benchmark. The gain comes from querying a database instead of recalling.

Jin et al. 2024, GeneGPT, Bioinformatics, doi:10.1093/bioinformatics/btae075

Same benchmark, same class of model. Allowing the model to call NCBI E-utilities moved the average from 0.12 to 0.83. The lesson is not to use GeneGPT specifically; it is that database lookup beats model memory for facts.

Reasoning helps, but it is not an explanation

Helps

Step-by-step prompting improves multi-step tasks. Majority voting over several reasoning paths helps further.

Wei et al. 2022 · Wang et al. 2023

But

With inputs biased toward wrong answers, models wrote plausible rationales without mentioning the bias. Accuracy fell by up to 36%.

Turpin et al. 2023, NeurIPS

Treat written reasoning as a draft argument to check. Never accept “are you sure?” as verification.

Chain-of-thought is useful for performance. But the text is not a faithful log of the computation. Asking the same model "are you sure?" invites it to flip because of sycophancy or to double down because of snowballing.

Demo 3 · PubChem, RDKit

Molecules: compare structures, not strings

Claimed structureResult (InChIKey vs PubChem)
caffeine, paracetamol, ibuprofencorrect
“aspirin” written as the methyl esterdifferent molecule
levodopa with the wrong stereocentre (D-DOPA)same skeleton, different stereo
C1=CC=CC=C1C(=Oinvalid SMILES

Ask for a name or PubChem CID, then look the structure up. Do not ask the model to write SMILES from memory.

Identical molecules have identical InChIKeys however the SMILES is written. The first block encodes the skeleton, the second the stereochemistry, which is how the D-DOPA error is caught. Side note: PubChem renamed its SMILES fields in 2025, so code written from older documentation can request fields that no longer exist.

Three lessons

Absent ≠ false

Open-world assumption: “not in KG” mixes true and false claims.

Present ≠ true

Curated and crowd-sourced graphs contain errors.

Context matters

“Treats” depends on approval, population, source and version. Record provenance; triangulate.

Related: Beniwal & Färber, From Universal Knowledge Graphs to Contextual Semantic Contracts, ISWC 2026

Use knowledge graphs to structure and constrain LLM output, keep provenance (source, version, date), and confirm high-stakes facts in at least two independent curated sources.

Part 4 · Inside the model

Where do facts live, and can we fix them?

Knowledge neurons

Neurons whose activation tracks a fact; editing them updates or erases it. Dai et al. 2022, ACL

ROME

Factual recall localised to mid-layer MLPs; a rank-one edit changes one association. Meng et al. 2022, NeurIPS

Cross-lingual editing

Editing methods show clear limits when an edit must carry across languages, especially across script families. Beniwal et al., Findings of EACL 2024

Where does toxicity live?

Localised to layers and neurons, suppressed by scaling or rank-one edits; concentrated in early MLP layers. Beniwal & Singh 2026, preprint

Cross-lingual editing used BLOOM, mBERT and XLM-RoBERTa with Latin-script and Indic-script languages. The toxicity work evaluated five models, two benchmarks and 90 configurations, and found that single-evaluator setups underestimate toxicity, so one judge is not enough. Practical message: a correction made in one language may not reach the same knowledge in another.

Reliability is not language-neutral

17

In practice · Prompting

A prompt that makes answers checkable

Task: Extract all reported binding affinities from the abstract below.

Source: the abstract, pasted in full

Format: JSON with keys compound, target, value, unit, quote

Rules: quote copied exactly; null if a field is not stated

Exclude: nothing from memory or other sources

Also: ask for identifiers · use neutral wording · re-run important questions · log model, version, date and prompt.

Five parts: task, source, format, uncertainty rules, exclusions. Neutral wording means asking for evidence for and against, not "explain why X is true". Templates are in the cheat sheet and the guidelines document.

In practice · Safe use

Using AI safely in research

Data

Genetic and health data are special categories under GDPR Art. 9(1). No patient data, unpublished results or credentials in consumer chatbots; use local or institution-approved tools.

Authorship

AI tools are not authors. Humans are accountable for all content. Disclose which tools, for what, and to what extent.

Peer review

EU: refrain from substantial use. DFG (since 16 April 2026): supporting use only, in confidential systems, disclosed.

Integrity

Never use AI to fabricate, falsify or manipulate data. Read and test generated code; verify every package.

EU Living guidelines, 3rd version, May 2026 · DFG statement 2023 · DFG Vordruck 4.04 (03/26) · ICMJE · GDPR Art. 9

All four boxes are taken from primary documents: the European Commission's living guidelines (third version, May 2026), the DFG statement of 2023 and its reviewer guideline in force since 16 April 2026, the ICMJE recommendations, and GDPR Article 9. Journals add their own rules; read your target journal's policy.

Take home

Seven rules

1 Ask for identifiers, not names.

5 Sample more than once; agreement is not proof.

2 Ask for JSON, then verify every field.

6 KG absence ≠ false; presence ≠ true.

3 Demand exact quotes; read the near-matches.

7 Report AI use like an instrument.

4 Allow abstention; count it separately.

If you remember one thing: make claims checkable, then check them. Model, version, date, prompts and what you verified against what belong in your methods section.

Reporting AI use: an illustrative methods statement

“We used Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic; API; 1–14 October 2026) to extract gene–disease associations from 120 PubMed abstracts. Prompts are provided in Supplementary File S2. Every extracted association was checked for an exact supporting quote and against NCBI Gene (accessed 15 October 2026); 9 of 412 items were corrected and 14 removed. The authors take full responsibility for the content.”

Name the model and version, dates, task, where the prompts are, what was verified against which source, and what changed.

This example is illustrative; the numbers show the level of detail, not a real study. The EU guidelines recommend naming the tool, version and date, and sharing prompts and outputs where relevant.
